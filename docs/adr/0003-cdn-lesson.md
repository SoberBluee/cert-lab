Where the build differs from the plan below:

- **Revalidation's claim** is measured as "origin bytes more than halved, and the edge hit ratio up by over 30 points" on a short-TTL docs site. The planned "under 20% of the bytes" can't hold, because first-time fetches still need the whole file.
- **The origin shield's test** uses the all-static docs site: in the web shop, carts and prices bypass caching and blur the effect.
- **Each city stands for 50 users,** so a cached cart can be served to someone else in the same city: that's where the leak happens.
- **Edges collapse concurrent misses** for the same file (collapsed forwarding, as real CDNs do), so the origin isn't flooded by the same miss.

## Update, 2026-10-23: step 4, missions tuned

`logic/cdnLesson.ts` holds the chapters, the eight missions and five freeze frames, and `logic/cdnLesson.test.ts` tests them. Each mission's starting setup and tempting answer fail and its solution passes on seed 1, and the solution and tempting answer give the same verdicts on seeds 2–4. Each pass mark sits in a clear gap measured across 10–20 seeds. No mission was dropped. Changes from the table below:

- **Everyone under 80 ms (was 100), on medians (was p95).** p95 can never pass, because cold misses to Virginia dominate it. At 100 ms, Dubai serving Singapore lands at 100.4 ms, a knife edge. At 80 ms the answer is exactly one edge per region, on every seed.
- **Cut origin egress: at most 15% of what users download (was 10%).** Over a 60-second run, only a long TTL plus the shield passes (3–11% across seeds); shield plus revalidation at a 10 s TTL sits at 18–32%.
- **Ship v2: "an edge hit ratio of at least 50%" replaces the egress budget.** Egress swings too much between seeds (video segments dominate it). Purge and versioned names score 57–77%; TTLs of 5 s or less reach 44% at most.
- **Don't leak the cart: "edge hit ratio of at least 45%" replaces the p95 condition.** The tempting fix, caching everything with a 1 s TTL, sometimes leaks nothing but has a hit ratio of only 8–13%.
- **Survive an outage: the edge dies at 15 s, with at most 12 failures over 30 s at 2 requests a second.** DNS answers are now cached per user rather than per city (closer to reality, and much less noisy). That gives a clean gap: anycast and TTLs of 10 s or less fail at most 11, while TTLs of 30 s or more fail at least 17.
- **The docs site** puts its users only in cities with an edge, so a push CDN's p95 is a flat 7 ms; San Francisco's 73 ms through New York would otherwise fail any tight bar.

## Context

ADR-0002 built the Stage (a deterministic event engine drawn as a live diagram) and the caching lesson on it. The CDN lesson is next in its build order. ADR-0002 only sketched it in a paragraph; this ADR designs it.

**What the sources cover:**

| Karan Pratap Singh | System Design Primer |
|---|---|
| A CDN is a geographically spread group of servers serving static content near users | The same, plus: DNS tells clients which server to contact |
| Origin vs edge locations; the UK user served from London instead of the US | Two wins: users are close, and the origin doesn't serve what the CDN does |
| Push CDNs: you upload on change; content is placed once, storage is maximised | Push: you rewrite URLs to point at the CDN |
| Pull CDNs: fetched on first request, then cached; good for heavy traffic | Pull: the first request is slower; a TTL decides how long content stays, and expired-but-unchanged files get pulled again for nothing |
| Disadvantages: extra charges, restrictions (blocked CDN domains), location (no PoP near your audience) | Disadvantages: cost, stale content until the TTL expires, URL changes |

**What this lesson adds beyond them**, because each one shows up on the map and is a common real-world mistake (flagged in the UI as "beyond the reading"):

- **Revalidation (304 Not Modified):** the fix for the Primer's "pulled again for nothing".
- **Origin shield (tiered caching):** why more PoPs can mean more origin pulls, and the fix.
- **Cache keys and personalised pages:** caching `/cart` hands one user's cart to the next user.
- **Failover and DNS TTL:** a dead PoP keeps getting traffic until cached DNS answers expire (ties back to the DNS lesson).
- **Connection reuse:** even a miss is faster through a nearby edge, because the edge keeps a warm connection to the origin (ties back to TCP and UDP).

**Constraints from Phase 1:**

- The Stage draws fixed boxes joined by straight wires. A map needs a background, curved routes, and pins that move.
- `CachingLab.tsx` is 495 lines, and about half of that is orchestration: playback, stepping, freeze frames, the mission flow and progress. A second lesson must not copy it.
- Mission thresholds have to be tuned with headless sweeps across seeds. Phase 1 dropped the layers mission because its results varied too much between seeds; the same rule applies here.
- No new runtime dependencies, and it all works offline.

## Decision

1. **The CDN lesson is a world map on the Stage.** It shows the origin, edge PoPs and user pins at real cities, with requests travelling curved routes. Latency comes from real great-circle distances.
2. **Before any CDN code, extract what the two lessons share:** a generic lesson model (chapters, missions, freeze frames, `runMission`), a `useLessonRunner` hook holding the orchestration out of `CachingLab`, and the database queue as a `Server` helper. The caching tests must pass unchanged afterwards.
3. **The Stage gains four features, all optional so caching is unaffected:** a background layer, curved wires, drawing only some wires, and draggable nodes.
4. **The land mask is generated once and committed** as a compact string, from Natural Earth's public-domain 110m land data. It's a script output, not a runtime dependency.
5. **Pins snap to a fixed list of 13 cities.** That keeps every route precomputable and every mission reproducible, while still letting you move people around the world.

**Map.**

- An equirectangular projection, cropped to 75°N to 55°S, so the stage is about 820 × 300.
- Land is a grid of dim dots. Routes are drawn as arcs between cities that stay inside the map (no wrapping at the date line); latency always uses the true great-circle distance.

**Nodes.**

- **Origin:** a box at Ashburn (us-east). It shows its objects with version badges, plus a load gauge.
- **PoPs:** teal hexagons at 8 candidate cities: New York, São Paulo, London, Frankfurt, Dubai, Singapore, Tokyo and Sydney. Each shows a ring of chips for its cached objects (version and TTL ring) and its hit ratio. Click one to open the **edge inspector**, a side panel with its full cache drawn like the caching lesson's slot grid.
- **Origin shield:** a mid-tier cache near the origin, shown when enabled.
- **User pins:** person markers at cities, each with a live p50 latency badge (green under 50 ms, amber under 150 ms, red above). Drag a pin to another city and it snaps there; add or remove pins from the city list.

**Latency model.**

- **RTT:** `2 × distance ÷ 200 km/ms × 1.6 + 5 ms`, i.e. light in fibre, real routes about 1.6× the great-circle distance, and a little per-hop overhead. That gives California → Netherlands → California at 145 ms (the Primer's latency table says about 150) and Sydney ↔ Ashburn at 256 ms. With no PoP closer than Singapore, Sydney users are still at 106 ms, which is why the first mission needs a PoP in Sydney itself.
- **Server time:** about 2 ms for an edge to serve from cache, and about 20 ms (static) or 80 ms (dynamic) at the origin, plus queueing when the origin is busy (the shared `Server` helper).
- **Handshakes (toggle):** with handshakes counted, a new connection costs extra round trips. A PoP keeps a warm connection to the origin, so only the short user ↔ PoP leg pays them.
- **On-screen travel time** is compressed (`250 + 1100 × √(distance ÷ 20,000 km)` ms), so a Sydney dot still visibly takes about twice as long as a London one without crawling.

**Content.**

| Object | Size | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| `logo.png` | 20 KB | static, rarely changes |
| `app.js` | 200 KB | static, changes on each deploy |
| `video/seg-1…40` | 2 MB each | static, long tail of popularity |
| `/api/prices` | 5 KB | dynamic, the same for everyone, changes every few seconds |
| `/cart` | 8 KB | personalised: different for every user |

Dot size follows payload size, so a video segment is visibly heavier than an API call.

**Cost** is in illustrative **credits**, not real prices: CDN egress, origin egress and edge storage are shown separately, so the lesson shows the trade-offs without claiming that a CDN always saves money.

**Push vs pull.**

- **Pull** (default): edges fetch on a miss and keep the object for its TTL. When the TTL runs out, an edge either refetches the whole object or, with revalidation on, sends a small conditional request; the origin answers 304 (a tiny dot) if the object hasn't changed.
- **Push:** "Publish" fans copies out from the origin to every PoP. Pushed objects never miss, and every PoP pays storage for every object, whether anyone there asks for it or not.

## Chapters

1. **Distance is latency.** Pins in five cities and the origin in Ashburn, with the CDN off: every arc runs to Virginia and Sydney's badge reads about 255 ms. Turn the CDN on and arcs go to the nearest PoP. The first request at each edge carries on to the origin, and the ones after it turn round at the edge. PoPs can be switched on and off on the map. Then the handshakes toggle: even a miss gets faster through the edge, because the slow leg reuses a warm connection.
2. **Pull, TTL and the origin.** Turn on all eight PoPs and spread pins everywhere, and the origin gets pulls from every edge. A TTL slider shows expiries as rings running out. Revalidation turns most refetches into tiny 304s, and the origin shield funnels all the edges' misses through one cache. Instruments: edge hit ratio, origin egress, the credits meter.
3. **Shipping changes.** "Deploy v2" bumps `app.js` at the origin, and PoPs keep version badges: with a long TTL users get v1 (⚠) for a while. The fixes are a purge (fan-out invalidation dots), a short TTL (costs origin trips), or versioned file names (`app.3f2a.js` is a new URL, so it's never stale). This chapter also covers push vs pull, with "Publish" and the storage meter.
4. **What not to cache, and when edges fail.** A caching rule picks what to cache: everything, static only, or whatever `Cache-Control` says. Cache everything, and the London PoP serves Alice's `/cart` to Bob (a red "leak" burst). Then kill a PoP and pick how users are steered to PoPs: DNS (with a TTL slider) keeps sending users to the dead PoP until their cached answer expires, while anycast reroutes in about 2 seconds. Finally, a country that blocks the CDN's domain (Karan's "restrictions") makes its users fall back to the origin.
5. **Sandbox.** Everything unlocked, including the second CDN provider, which has no South American PoP (see the "Should you use a CDN?" mission).

## Teaching-claim tests

- Latency grows with distance: San Francisco ↔ Sydney is slower than San Francisco ↔ London, and Sydney ↔ Ashburn's modeled RTT is between 220 and 280 ms.
- Once warm, Sydney's p50 behind a Sydney PoP is under 40 ms.
- A cold miss through an edge is no faster than going direct without handshakes counted, and is faster with them.
- With revalidation, unchanged objects cost the origin under 20% of the bytes that full refetches would.
- Push never misses on pushed objects, and storage equals objects × PoPs.
- The origin shield cuts origin pulls to roughly one per object per TTL, whatever the number of PoPs.
- After a deploy: versioned names give zero stale responses, while a long TTL without a purge stays stale until expiry.
- Caching everything leaks `/cart`, and the "static only" rule leaks nothing.
- With a dead PoP, DNS steering fails requests for roughly the DNS TTL, and anycast only for its convergence time.
- Users with no PoP near them get slower with the CDN on.

## Trade-offs and consequences

- **Snapped pins vs free placement.** Free dragging would be more playful, but it makes every route dynamic and every mission depend on where you dropped a pin. Thirteen cities covers every continent and keeps runs reproducible.
- **Drawn arcs vs true great circles.** Arcs that never wrap the date line are readable. The latency is still the true distance, and the caption says so.
- **Credits, not dollars.** Real CDN and cloud prices vary and change. Credits keep the shape of the trade-off (egress, storage, origin load) without teaching wrong numbers.
- **Extraction first** costs time before anything CDN-shaped appears, but every later lesson (proxy, clustering, scalability, availability, storage) reuses it.
- **Eight missions** is the most of any lesson so far. The two push/pull missions are deliberately opposite, like the scan and trending missions in caching.
